Capping the Interstate – Steel Truss Cap

CE 2200 Statics · Vanderbilt University · Fall 2023

In this team project I designed, analyzed, fabricated, and tested a steel truss prototype for a proposed “cap” over Interstate 40 at Jefferson Street in Nashville. The goal was to reconnect communities separated by the Federal Highway System and to explore how structural engineering decisions have real-life implications.

Working from first principles, I developed a statically determinate truss, predicted its failure modes using both yielding and Euler buckling criteria, then built and load-tested a welded steel prototype. I used the results to design community bridge load layouts that are structurally efficient and resilient to real-world uncertainties.

Context & Design Goals

The project was inspired by Nashville’s efforts to cap Interstate 40 and repair the damage done to Jefferson Street’s historically Black neighborhoods when the highway was first constructed. My objectives were to:

  • Research how the Federal-Aid Highway Act and interstate routing impacted URM communities.
  • Design a repeatable steel truss module that could span the highway and support a new green space.
  • Predict member forces and failure modes using statics and stability theory.
  • Fabricate a lab-scale prototype with welded and bolted connections and test it to failure.
  • Translate results into safe load layouts for community buildings, landscaping, and play areas.

Preliminary Truss Design & Analysis

I began with a symmetric Pratt-style truss designed for a 300 lb central point load, representing part of the surface park and pedestrian traffic. Using the method of joints and method of sections, I solved for axial forces in each member and verified the results with a Johns Hopkins online truss calculator. The analytic and simulation results matched, confirming the internal force diagram used for subsequent design work.

Member force prediction

For the 300 lb design load I computed axial forces in all members and classified them as tension or compression. This established which members would be governed by yielding and which would be governed by Euler buckling.

  • Symmetric span with central load.
  • Statics: ∑Fx, ∑Fy, ∑M = 0.
  • Hand calcs checked against truss software.

Failure criteria

For each member I compared allowable axial stress (σall = N/A) to material yield strength and calculated critical buckling loads using Euler’s formula:

  • σall < σy for yielding.
  • Pcr = π²EI / (kL)² with pin-pin members (k = 1).
  • Two cross-sections: solid flat bars and channel sections.

Manufacturing the Steel Truss Prototype

To validate the analysis, I fabricated a lab-scale steel truss using flat bar and channel stock, cut connection plates, and a mix of welded and bolted pin connections.

Member & connection prep

I cut bar stock for the top and bottom chords, diagonals, and verticals, then machined small connection plates with centered ¼-inch bolt holes to act as pin bars. Slots were cut in the members and plates so they could be welded together while maintaining alignment.

  • 1/8" × 1" bar stock for most members.
  • Channel section for the top chord.
  • Plates sized to maintain pin spacing and edge distance.

Welding sequence & distortion control

To avoid thermal warping, I used short welds, alternating sides, and allowed the assembly to cool between phases.

  • Phase 1 – Weld plates to member ends using magnets for alignment.
  • Phase 2 – Weld pin bars to the top channel and bottom chord with 10" spacing.
  • Phase 3 – Insert members between plate pairs and secure with ¼" bolts.

Careful weld sequencing preserved the intended geometry so the prototype matched the analytical model as closely as possible.

Bolting & connection behavior

Bolts were tightened enough to remove play but not so tight that they behaved as fully fixed connections. Over-tightening would effectively reduce the effective length factor k, increasing buckling capacity but also altering the load path away from the ideal pin-joint model.

Structural Calculations & Predicted Failure

Using measured cross-sections, I computed moments of inertia for both the flat-bar and channel members, then evaluated buckling and yielding capacities member-by-member.

  • Computed I for each section from composite area formulas.
  • Evaluated Pcr for all compression members and σall for all members.
  • Identified controlling buckling member and controlling tension member.

For the prototype geometry the controlling theoretical limit was buckling of one compression member at approximately 1,644 N (≈370 lb), while yielding did not control until over 20 kN. This suggested the truss should fail by buckling well before any member yielded.

Lab Testing & Failure Analysis

I tested the prototype using a rope-and-winch apparatus that applied a vertical load at the central top joint, with the ends supported on wood frames. Load was measured with an inline scale until the truss failed.

Measured performance

  • Failure load ≈ 538 lb.
  • Observed inelastic buckling of the second diagonal member from one end.
  • No visible yielding of members or fracture at welds.

The prototype exceeded the predicted buckling load but still failed by compression instability rather than material yield, validating the choice of failure mode.

Explaining the discrepancy

The higher-than-predicted failure load was likely due to partially fixed connections created by bolt preload and weld restraint. This effectively reduced the effective length of some compression members (k < 1), increasing their buckling capacity while also redistributing forces away from the idealized statics model.

The test highlighted how sensitive real trusses are to connection details and why conservative assumptions are important in design.

Community Bridge Layout & Load Combinations

To translate the prototype into a conceptual bridge, I considered three surface loads per module: a small community building, landscaped area, and playground. Each was converted to an equivalent nodal load on the truss and combined into six possible configurations.

Statistical load balancing

For each configuration I solved the member forces and computed the standard deviation of axial loads. Lower standard deviation indicated a more even load distribution (no single “overworked” member).

  • Loads #1 and #5: most even distribution, best use of structural capacity.
  • Loads #2 and #6: acceptable but less uniform.
  • Loads #3 and #4: least uniform; still safe but with less margin.

Safety margins

Using the prototype’s controlling buckling and tension capacities as a reference, I checked each configuration:

  • Weakest compression member remained >100 lb below buckling.
  • Weakest tension member remained >4,000 lb below yield.
  • No configuration approached failure, even before adding live-load factors.

Based on this analysis I recommended using the Load #1 / #5 layouts, which place the playground near midspan to share load efficiently across the truss.
